## Summary

An unsolicited email claims to come from Chief Justice Stuart Rabner and says a supposed compensation fund worth “$50 million per month” is about to be canceled. It claims “USA Bank,” the World Bank, and the President were involved and that an alleged $500 charge was reduced to $25 so the recipient can secure or release the funds.

**Verdict:** Confirmed scam pattern. The message impersonates a real judge, invents a government/bank compensation process, and uses a small advance fee to lure the recipient with an impossible payout.

## Verification and contradictions

1. **Wrong role and court:** New Jersey Courts identifies Stuart Rabner as Chief Justice of the Supreme Court of New Jersey. The email confusingly presents him as connected to the U.S. Supreme Court.
2. **Free-email mismatch:** The message comes from a personal Gmail address, not an official court or government domain.
3. **Malformed borrowed address:** The U.S. Supreme Court’s official address is 1 First Street, NE, Washington, DC 20543. The email writes “NY Washington” and uses the federal court’s address while claiming a New Jersey judicial identity.
4. **Impossible payout:** A surprise payment of $50 million every month is not credible.
5. **Advance-fee mechanism:** The requested $25 is framed as necessary to prevent cancellation or release a vastly larger payment.
6. **Fabricated institutions/documents:** “USA Bank” and “Release Approval Order Certificate (RAOC)” are used as official-sounding props without verifiable case, docket, agency, or program details.
7. **Authority stacking:** A state chief justice, the U.S. Supreme Court, the President, “USA Bank,” and the World Bank are combined to manufacture legitimacy.
8. **FTC-consistent scam pattern:** Government impersonators use official names and pressure targets to pay or disclose information. A government official will not demand payment to release unexpected compensation.

## Detection signals to incorporate

Increase scam risk sharply when these signals occur together:

- Judge, justice, court, federal agency, President, World Bank, or government-compensation language sent from a free-mail domain.
- Phrases such as `compensation fund`, `placed on my desk for cancellation`, `secure your fund`, `release approval order`, `release certificate`, or `legal protection/security claim`.
- A small requested fee paired with a huge promised payment.
- Claims that a judge or President personally intervened in an individual payout.
- Mixed or contradictory judicial identities, such as a state chief justice represented as part of the U.S. Supreme Court.
- Official-looking address text that does not match the claimed institution.
- “Humanitarian reasons,” “compassion,” cancellation, or urgent release language near a payment demand.

## Recommended user action

- Do not reply.
- Do not pay the $25 or any later “release,” tax, insurance, delivery, certificate, or processing fee.
- Do not provide identification, banking information, phone number, address, verification codes, or signatures.
- Mark as phishing/spam and block the sender.
- Preserve the original email and full headers if reporting.
- Report to the FTC at `ReportFraud.ftc.gov` and to Google/Gmail as phishing.
